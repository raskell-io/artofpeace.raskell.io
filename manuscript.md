# The Art of Peace

by Raffael Schneider

*First manuscript*

## Before the shore

The thought of this book came to me during a morning swim in Nago Bay. I was thinking about how much I love *The Art of War*, and wondering what I might learn by turning my attention to the preservation of peace.

I work with computers and build things. My habits follow me beyond my work. I ask what happens when a promise fails, when two people understand the same words differently, when something entrusted to one pair of hands becomes too important to lose.

These questions led me here. I want to understand how people can disagree without becoming enemies, protect themselves without seeking domination, and share without surrendering their independence.

I love harmony. I also want room for the person who interrupts it with a necessary question. A room in which everyone speaks alike may be a room of friends. It may also be a room in which someone has learned to be afraid.

The peace I seek has room for an unwelcome answer. It allows people to remain different, to change their minds, and sometimes to part. It asks restraint of those who possess power, and offers protection to those whose refusal would otherwise carry no weight.

I have written these passages as things to practise. Some concern the conduct of a single person; others concern arrangements people build together. A household, a workshop, and a community carry different responsibilities. I would examine a principle again before carrying it from one to another.

I do not expect to settle the meaning of peace. I hope to become more useful in its keeping.

## I. On peace

### 1

You may sit beneath the same tree and look toward different mountains. Offer shade without first requiring your neighbour to admire the view you have chosen. There is room beneath the branches for a disagreement neither of you will resolve.

### 2

Ask what a quiet place permits. Can someone question its customs, refuse its demands, or depart without being pursued? Attend to the answer before you praise its tranquillity. Silence tells you little until you know what it costs to break it.

### 3

Peace requires some agreements: that a refusal has meaning, that injury calls for an answer, that strength grants no ownership of another person. Beyond these necessities, leave space. A common life need not occupy the whole of anyone who enters it.

### 4

Begin with the people who bear the cost of an arrangement. Those who enjoy the quiet may not hear the labour that maintains it. Ask who swallows a grievance, who always yields, and who has ceased expecting to be consulted. Their answers belong in your account of how well things are going.

### 5

Attend to a small grievance while it can still be spoken plainly. When every complaint must become a crisis before anyone listens, people learn to bring crises. Give ordinary speech a hearing, and a person need not first become dangerous to be taken seriously.

### 6

You cannot promise that no one will ever cause harm. You can arrange for harm to be noticed, interrupted, and answered. Build with this modest ambition. Much suffering begins when people defend the perfection of an arrangement against the testimony of those living within it.

### 7

There are disputes worth entering. To protect someone from coercion may disturb a comfortable peace. Ask what your intervention protects, what injury it may cause, and how it can end. The wish to avoid conflict does not release you from the care of another person.

### 8

Let peace return something to those who keep it: an afternoon without vigilance, a friendship without calculation, time to make something that serves no quarrel.

## II. On difference

### 1

Do not make agreement the price of belonging. People may share a table while disagreeing about how a life should be lived. Establish what their conduct at the table requires. Leave their inward lives in their own keeping.

### 2

The dissenter need not become a visionary to deserve a hearing. Leave room for the person who is simply mistaken. You have stood there too.

### 3

When a group agrees quickly, ask each person what they noticed before the agreement formed. A quiet reservation can disappear beneath the relief of belonging. Make room for it while the decision is still open, especially when the most powerful person has already spoken.

### 4

You owe a person consideration; you do not owe their claim your assent. Examine it. Ask for reasons. Offer yours without making the speaker's humiliation your reward. An argument can be severe in its scrutiny and careful with the person who has brought it.

### 5

Do not require affection as proof of peaceful intent. Some people will live well beside you while keeping their distance. Let courtesy, reliable conduct, and a willingness to settle shared matters carry what intimacy cannot. A neighbour may remain a stranger without becoming a threat.

### 6

Protect time and space in which people can think beyond the group. Let a person walk a little way with an unfamiliar thought before asking where it leads. Some questions need solitude before they can bear an audience.

### 7

Difference does not relieve anyone of obligations they have freely undertaken. A shared workshop may require that its tools be returned and its dangers marked. Such requirements should answer to the work and the people affected. They need not extend into a prescribed way of life.

### 8

When two ways of living cannot share a particular space, seek an arrangement that preserves as much freedom as possible: different hours, different rooms, or a fair division of responsibilities. Sometimes people must part. Do not make the failure to live closely together a reason they must live as enemies.

## III. On boundaries

### 1

Know what you will defend, and make it known before anger speaks for you. An unspoken limit may feel obvious from within your own life. Another person approaches it from elsewhere. Where it is safe to do so, give them words before the encounter becomes a wound.

### 2

Your body, attention, and private life are yours to govern. Your discomfort does not grant you the same authority over another person's conscience. Examine the reach of what you demand. If your protection requires that everyone think as you do, there will be no end to your territory.

### 3

Consent belongs to a particular act, under particular conditions. Yesterday's welcome does not settle today's invitation. Allow people to alter the access they grant you. Where commitments must change, discuss the consequences without treating a former promise as ownership of the person who made it.

### 4

Distinguish an unwelcome opinion from a threat, and criticism from a campaign to make someone afraid. Look at conduct, repetition, power, and the ability to withdraw. Neither the claim of offence nor the claim of free inquiry can settle the matter by itself.

### 5

A shared boundary needs a shared hearing. The fence between two gardens cannot be placed by measuring from one doorway alone. Walk the ground together. Notice the old path, the shade, the water that passes beneath the proposed division. Hear those whose lives the boundary will alter. When agreement fails, seek a fair means of deciding that neither party alone controls.

### 6

Explain the purpose of a rule, the conduct it addresses, and how a mistaken judgment may be challenged. A boundary that moves whenever its keeper is displeased teaches vigilance rather than respect. People should be able to discover what is required without studying the keeper's moods.

### 7

When a boundary is crossed, protect what is at risk. Use the restraint sufficient to stop the intrusion, and examine it as circumstances change. An offence does not give you an unlimited claim upon the offender. Anger often asks for more territory than safety requires.

### 8

Keep a gate where a gate is possible. People change, needs change, and an old boundary may outlive its purpose. Review it without shame. You may also find that it remains necessary. An open heart does not oblige you to leave an unsafe door unlocked.

## IV. On strength

### 1

Cultivate enough strength to protect a refusal. This may mean learning a skill, setting something aside, finding allies, or building a means of leaving. A choice becomes more real when a person can survive the displeasure of someone who would rather choose for them.

### 2

Do not demand that the vulnerable become formidable before they deserve protection. Put some strength within their reach: a witness who will attend, a place to go, a procedure that answers. The usefulness of your own security includes what it allows you to offer someone without it.

### 3

Prepare your capabilities more carefully than you display them. A continual demonstration of power occupies everyone's attention with the question of what you may do next. Make your limits and intentions understandable. Let preparedness give you the patience to hear an answer you dislike.

### 4

When you hold power, give people a way to decline your invitation. Then observe how you treat the first person who uses it.

### 5

Bind your strength to purposes that others can examine. Name who may act, under what conditions, and who may call the action to account. Good intentions offer little shelter when an injury has already occurred and the person responsible is also the sole judge.

### 6

In immediate danger, protect life and make escape possible. Take no pleasure in the force that may be required. Once the danger has passed, the authority granted by that moment must be examined again. Urgency is a poor foundation for a permanent right to command.

### 7

An ally should enlarge your freedom to act wisely. Be wary of loyalty that requires you to inherit every quarrel or excuse every injury. Promise what you can honour. Keep the judgment necessary to tell a friend when their own conduct endangers what you have joined to protect.

### 8

Strength is also the ability to remain useful after a setback. Keep knowledge in more than one mind, care in more than one pair of hands, and a route home when the usual road is closed. People with alternatives have less reason to make every loss a final battle.

## V. On understanding

### 1

Before you answer a demand, learn what the other person believes will happen if it is refused. The words may concern a small thing; the fear beneath them may concern a livelihood, a place among others, or a previous injury. Understanding that fear gives you something more precise to address.

### 2

Ask questions whose answers could change your conduct. A question used only to expose foolishness is an accusation wearing borrowed clothes. If you already know what you will do whatever the answer, say what you intend and do not call the performance listening.

### 3

Distinguish what happened, what you infer, and what you fear may follow. Speak each in its own name. Two people can agree upon an event and disagree about its meaning. Keeping those matters separate leaves room to investigate without requiring either person to surrender their whole account at once.

### 4

Try to state another person's concern in words they recognise before answering it. Their recognition does not prove the concern justified. It tells you that your answer is approaching the matter they brought. Many disputes grow while each side diligently refutes something the other did not mean.

### 5

Understanding may reveal deliberate harm. You need not invent an innocent motive to preserve your patience. Learn the motive as accurately as you can, then act upon the danger. Explanation can guide a response without becoming an excuse or an invitation to endure more injury.

### 6

Leave some things unknown. You can share a wall without possessing the key to every room. Ask for the knowledge necessary to settle what you share, and let the rest remain in another person's keeping.

### 7

Examine the story you most enjoy telling about an adversary. Notice whether every action confirms it: their silence proves guilt, their answer proves deceit, their concession proves weakness. An account that cannot be corrected will eventually guide you away from the person standing before you.

### 8

Keep a means of learning when you have understood badly. Invite correction, compare recollections, and return to uncertain matters. An error admitted early may cost a little pride. Defended long enough, it can gather promises, allies, and injuries that make correction much harder for everyone.

## VI. On sharing

### 1

Learn the difference between what is spent by giving and what may grow through use. Food must be replenished. A method can be taught to many hands. Share with attention to these differences, so that generosity continues after the first grateful gathering has gone home.

### 2

Teach in a way that permits the learner to continue without you. Place the tool in their hands. Explain what makes it useful and where it can cause harm. Let them practise, ask, and alter the method. One day they may use it well in a way you would never have chosen. Leave them that freedom with the gift.

### 3

Ask what a gift will require of its receiver. Something costly to store, maintain, or repay may arrive as a burden. Offer a refusal without offence. Generosity begins with attention to another person's life, including the possibility that your offering has no place in it.

### 4

Let a gift end where the receiver's freedom begins. If you expect repayment, name the terms before it is accepted. Keep no hidden account of obedience owed for kindness.

### 5

Before disputing a shortage, examine what makes the thing scarce. A tool might be shared at different hours; a skill might be taught; waste might be reduced. Some limits will remain. Meet those limits honestly, with fair terms, instead of promising an abundance whose costs someone else must bear.

### 6

Care for the common well includes care for those who clean it. Make the necessary work visible and distribute its burden. A resource can be open to everyone while its maintenance quietly exhausts a few. Attend to their rest before calling the arrangement generous.

### 7

Share knowledge with regard for those it can expose. Another person's confidence is not yours to offer as a contribution. A method that enables harm asks for judgment about how and with whom it is shared. The wish to be open does not absolve you of care.

### 8

Let shared work have a future beyond its first keepers. Leave intelligible instructions, teach a successor, and permit useful changes. A gift becomes more durable when people can repair it without asking the giver's permission. Take pleasure in seeing it serve a purpose you did not foresee.

## VII. On trust

### 1

Begin with a promise small enough to keep and meaningful enough to matter. Return what you borrowed. Arrive when you said you would. Tell someone when a delay becomes likely. Trust gathers in ordinary events where a person could have been careless and chose to attend.

### 2

Trust a person for what you have reason to trust them with. Good judgment in one craft does not establish it in every matter. Affection does not make a memory exact. A limited trust can be both sincere and strong when its limits are understood by those who share it.

### 3

Agree how important promises will be checked. A measure accepted in advance can spare people the indignity of repeated accusation. Check what bears upon the promise, allow mistakes to be corrected, and end the inquiry where its purpose ends. Verification becomes domination when it claims an unlimited view.

### 4

Keep a record when memory alone would leave too much at stake. Write down the commitment, the conditions, and what will happen if circumstances change. Let each person inspect the same account. A clear record can protect a friendship from the work of deciding whose recollection deserves loyalty.

### 5

Arrange for bad news to travel early. If the first person to report a failure is punished for disturbing the peace, the next failure will arrive later and larger. Thank the messenger for the chance to act, then examine what happened and who bears responsibility.

### 6

After a broken promise, ask what failed: ability, understanding, circumstance, or willingness. These require different repairs. Someone may have promised more than they could carry, or understood a different task, or found the road closed. Someone may also have chosen to abandon you. More reassurance cannot supply a missing skill; a clearer agreement cannot restrain deliberate disregard. Let the next step answer the failure you actually found.

### 7

Do not make another person prove trust by abandoning every safeguard. A keeper may be honest and still become ill, be deceived, or make a mistake. Separate responsibilities where a single failure could do great harm. Good faith should have support when human attention falters.

### 8

Restore trust at the pace supported by conduct. An apology may open the way; it cannot determine how far another person must walk. Begin where the risk is bearable. Permit confidence to return unevenly, and accept that some responsibilities may remain elsewhere.

## VIII. On diplomacy

### 1

Before a difficult meeting, decide what must be protected and what you are merely accustomed to possessing. Confusing the two makes every adjustment appear a defeat. Carry a clear purpose into the room, with enough freedom around it to recognise an unexpected way forward.

### 2

Find a place where people can speak without performing for those who reward their anger. Agree who needs to be present and who must later hear the result. A private conversation can make candour possible; the resulting agreement still owes an account to the people it will bind.

### 3

Begin with what each person needs to continue. A demand names one proposed solution. The need beneath it may admit several. Ask what the demanded thing would make possible, and what other arrangements could do the same without placing the whole burden on someone else.

### 4

Know what the people at the table may promise. An agreement made beyond their authority will return as a fresh grievance when those absent refuse it. Include the people whose work, safety, or resources are required. Convenience is a poor reason to bargain with what belongs to them.

### 5

When you cannot settle the whole dispute, settle something worth settling. Keep that small agreement well. Let it be useful even if no larger agreement follows.

### 6

Leave room for a concession to be explained with dignity. A person who returns to their companions with nothing but public humiliation may struggle to keep even a wise agreement. You need not disguise the truth. Give the truth a form that permits continued cooperation.

### 7

Patience needs a measure. When talks consume time while harm continues, set conditions for continuing and attend to those exposed. A promise to speak again is useful only alongside conduct that gives speaking a chance. Some meetings should end so that protection can begin.

### 8

Before leaving, say plainly what has been agreed, what remains open, who will act, and when you will review the result. Include a way to report trouble. The care given to these ordinary details may preserve more peace than the eloquence with which the meeting began.

## IX. On escalation

### 1

Notice when your purpose changes. You may enter a dispute seeking a returned tool and find yourself seeking proof that the other person has always been unworthy. Return to the thing that needs settling. A grievance grows harder to repair when it becomes a judgment upon an entire life.

### 2

Slow the acts that cannot easily be undone. Draft the message and wait before sending it. Check the report before repeating it. Where immediate safety is not at stake, give your next action time to become a choice. Anger supplies urgency even when the matter can bear a night.

### 3

A pause should have a purpose and, where possible, a time of return. Say what you need to consider and when you will answer. Silence without explanation can feel like abandonment or a threat. Make the interval understandable, unless contact itself would place someone in danger.

### 4

Keep the dispute within its true extent. A failure in one undertaking need not sever every useful connection. Preserve the channels that remain safe: the shared task, the necessary notice, the means of asking for help. These may later carry the first attempt at repair.

### 5

Do not gather an audience merely to make retreat expensive. Public scrutiny can be necessary when private appeals fail or others face danger. Seek it for protection and accountability. An audience assembled for humiliation may soon demand a harsher outcome than the injured person needed.

### 6

Respond to what has been done and what credibly threatens to follow. Do not charge someone for every feared future at once. State what conduct must cease and what restraint you will observe if it does. A limit is easier to respect when its observance changes something.

### 7

Keep a way to send a message even when ordinary conversation has broken down. A trusted intermediary may carry a warning, a correction, or an offer that neither person can yet deliver calmly. Give that messenger a narrow task. They should not be forced to inherit the quarrel.

### 8

Stop when the necessary purpose has been achieved. Leave the last cruel word unsaid, even if you have thought of a good one.

## X. On humility

### 1

Hold your convictions firmly enough to act and openly enough to learn. Before a consequential decision, ask what evidence would lead you to reconsider. If the answer is nothing, examine whether you are protecting a judgment or a place for yourself beyond correction.

### 2

Make a place for the person who tells you that you are wrong. If you hold authority, their candour may cost them more than your invitation admits. Receive the first unwelcome answer with care. Others will learn from your conduct whether the invitation was real.

### 3

Admit uncertainty precisely. Say what you know, what you suspect, and what remains to be learned. You need not dissolve every conviction into doubt. A named uncertainty can be investigated; a concealed one may guide everyone's work while no one knows to examine it.

### 4

When you discover that you have given a wrong direction, go first to those still following it. The explanation of how you came to be mistaken can wait.

### 5

Let people improve an idea without pledging allegiance to its maker. If a useful suggestion becomes a contest over who first possessed it, the work may stop while pride is satisfied. Give credit accurately and allow the idea to travel beyond the conditions of your approval.

### 6

Beware of making your humility another demand for praise. You can listen without announcing your openness, yield without displaying your sacrifice, and learn without asking the teacher to admire your courage. Some of the room you make for others comes from occupying less of their attention.

### 7

Humility does not require you to surrender a well-founded objection because others sound certain. Ask again. Show your reasons. Remain willing to be answered. The quiet person who persists in a necessary question may serve the group better than someone who mistakes easy agreement for modesty.

### 8

Remember how much of your strength was given. Someone held you before you could stand. Someone taught you words with which you now disagree with them. You work with knowledge preserved by hands you will never touch. Let that memory temper what you claim for yourself, and guide what you place within another person's reach.

## XI. On what we build

### 1

Build for the day when someone is tired, frightened, careless, or tempted. A practice that works only while everyone is generous will be tested precisely when generosity is scarce. Put enough clarity and support around good intentions that a lapse need not become a lasting injury.

### 2

Let a mistake remain near the place where it can be repaired. A whole household should not hang from one forgotten errand. Preserve another way to reach help, enough reserve to recover, and knowledge in more than one pair of hands.

### 3

Examine what your arrangements reward. If concealment preserves a person's standing while honesty threatens it, speeches about integrity will have hard work to do. Make the responsible action practicable. People should not have to choose between telling the truth and meeting a need you could reasonably protect.

### 4

Give responsibility the means it requires. Someone asked to maintain a shared resource needs time, authority suited to the task, and a way to obtain help. Assigning a duty without these things places blame in advance. Ask what the keeper will need on an ordinary difficult day.

### 5

Allow the people affected by a rule to help examine it. Set a time for review and a means of appeal. A rule may have served well when it was made and become burdensome as circumstances changed. Preserve its purpose through revision when preserving its exact form would defeat it.

### 6

Make cooperation valuable without making departure ruinous. Shared work can deepen trust, yet complete dependence can turn a disagreement into a threat to survival. Keep records portable, obligations understandable, and alternatives possible. A person should have reasons to stay beyond the devastation of leaving.

### 7

When an arrangement repeatedly fails, examine it before asking for another sacrifice from those already carrying it. More vigilance may briefly conceal a poor design. Ask where the burden collects, what can be simplified, and which expectation no ordinary person can reliably meet.

### 8

Arrange for your own replacement. Teach the work, explain the difficult decisions, and let others practise while you are still available. If peace depends upon one indispensable keeper, their absence becomes everyone's danger. Leave something that can be tended by hands you will never know.

## XII. On repair

### 1

When harm has been done, attend first to the person exposed to it. Stop the conduct, meet urgent needs, and protect their freedom to speak or remain silent. A discussion of reconciliation has little worth while the injury continues or its account must be delivered under pressure.

### 2

Name what you did without making the injured person carry the burden of your intentions. Say what happened, what it cost, and what you can now do. An explanation may help establish how to prevent a recurrence. It should not be used to make the injury disappear from the account.

### 3

Offer repair in a form the injured person can actually use. Replace what was lost, correct what was said, return what was taken, or carry work your conduct imposed. Ask what would help. Do not require them to manage your remorse before they can receive your assistance.

### 4

Repair what you can without waiting to be forgiven. Forgiveness is another person's gift. Your responsibility does not depend upon receiving it.

### 5

A path back should include evidence of change. Set responsibilities that can be understood and examined, with protection proportionate to the remaining risk. The person returning needs a way to act differently. Those receiving them need more than an appeal to forget what they learned.

### 6

Some relationships can be repaired only at a distance. A person may wish you well and still decline your company. Accept the limit without treating it as revenge. Settle what remains between you. There may be no shared table again, and still there can be peace between the two houses.

### 7

Keep an honest memory of what occurred. Record enough to protect others and recognise repetition, while respecting the privacy of those harmed. Memory should help people judge present conduct accurately. Do not require either a permanent public spectacle or the convenient disappearance of the past.

### 8

When a repair has been made and a responsibility fulfilled, recognise it. If no changed conduct can ever alter your response, say plainly what relationship remains possible. Do not offer a road back whose destination moves whenever the traveller approaches. Hope also carries obligations for the person who offers it.

## XIII. On keeping peace

### 1

Return to your agreements before necessity forces the visit. Ask what has become difficult, what remains useful, and what people have stopped saying. A quiet conversation held regularly can receive matters that would arrive much later as accusations. Give maintenance a place among the things you value.

### 2

Make room for rest. Share the watch and leave some hours unclaimed. Do not spend all of a person's patience on unnecessary burdens and then ask why they have none left for a difficult day.

### 3

Let people meet in work and pleasure that do not require agreement about everything. Cook, build, learn, or tend something together. Such encounters need not dissolve a serious difference. They can preserve knowledge of the person beyond it, which a quarrel may otherwise steadily erase.

### 4

Teach disagreement where the consequences are still small. Let a child question a reason; let a newcomer suggest a change. Show how to answer without ridicule and how to refuse without contempt. People learn the permissible shape of conflict from the ordinary encounters around them.

### 5

Keep some room unassigned. A person you have not imagined will arrive with a question your arrangements cannot yet answer. Leave them a chair and a way to revise the plan.

### 6

Measure your peace in the freedoms people can actually use. Who can say no? Who can report an injury? Who can change their work, beliefs, or companions without losing every means of living? Return to these questions when orderly appearances tempt you to stop looking.

### 7

Allow those who come after you to question what you preserved. Give them your reasons, including the failures that taught you caution. They will face circumstances you cannot settle for them. Their independence is part of what your care was meant to make possible.

### 8

There will be another disagreement. Leave a place for it: a time to speak, a way to listen, a limit upon injury, and a means of repair. Then return to the life these things protect. There is work to do, someone to visit, and a morning in which you may enter the water with your thoughts unfinished.

## Returning

I began with the wish to write about peace. I find myself returning to ordinary acts: making a promise clear, hearing a refusal, teaching what I know, correcting what I have misunderstood.

I will not always do these things well. I would like to live among people who can tell me when I fail, and within arrangements that make it possible to answer them without first defending everything I have ever been.

That is something I can help build. I can leave room at a table, make a limit known, or place a useful tool in another person's hands. I can protect a disagreement from becoming an injury. When I cannot mend a relationship, I can try to leave it without adding cruelty.

I hope these pages remain open to the same examination they ask of other things. Keep what helps. Question what does not. Carry a better thought beyond them.
